# Which uncertainties could change the cloud result?

Whether an airborne particle retains light-sensitive compounds after evaporation makes the largest chemical difference in the clean July cloud. Whether an instrument can see the predicted droplet-size shift is a separate question. Matched chemistry, numerical and sampling experiments show where each uncertainty enters.

## Can the residue continue to react?

All chemical alternatives start with the same amount of fast-reacting material, the same fresh water affinity and the same dry size distribution. Each simulation uses the same July illumination and the same clean parcel rising at 2 m/s. In darkness it produces 159.3 droplets/cm³. Assigning the fastest photochemistry to different volatility classes changes the sun-aged cloud.

| Organic material with the fastest reaction | Sun-aged cloud-top droplets (cm⁻³) | Change in reflected sunlight (W/m²) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| More volatile parents | 146.0 | −1.03 |
| Parents distributed independently of volatility | 121.3 | −3.14 |
| Less volatile parents | 79.2 | −7.64 |
| More volatile parents, plus slow reaction of condensed first-generation products | 145.3 | −1.08 |

The fluxes are all-hour means above the modeled cloud and local surface, relative to the matched dark control. The film preconditioning experiment favors the first assignment. In the fourth case, every product that reacts again becomes permanent vapor, so the extra reaction can cost the particle dry mass. A product could also react while remaining condensed with essentially the same volatility and water affinity. Measuring chemical turnover alone would then give little information about cloud activation. [Film and airborne constraints](film-and-airborne.md) · [Bai supporting information](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.est.5c16671.s001)

A discriminating measurement would select 100 nm particles, evaporate them in darkness to half their initial dry mass, and measure parent response to a calibrated 330 nm exposure. The simulated action per unit remaining organic material was 39–43% of fresh for fastest reaction in more volatile compounds, 100% for independent rates and 157–159% for fastest reaction in less volatile compounds. A second experiment would track a preformed, labeled product in equal illuminated and dark samples. After 48 minutes at 30 times the reference lamp intensity, 0%, 2.43% or 9.35% of the model marker reacted under zero, quarter-strength or full slow-product action. The dark sample lost 11.84% of the intact marker to gas or exhaust. Collecting both particle and gas phases would distinguish chemical conversion from the loss of intact molecules. [Suspended-particle evaporation measurements](https://doi.org/10.1021/es505331d) · [Independent limonene film photochemical measurements](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208121119)

## Did the surviving droplets grow larger?

The July cohort analysis matched each original seed population across its dark-aged and sun-aged parcels. Restricting both outcomes to a 3.5–40 µm sampling window separated droplets present in both populations from those that entered or left. In the clean 0.25 m/s ascent, growth of the shared cohorts contributed +1.15 µm to the change in the full window’s number-median wet diameter. The net effect of entries and exits was +0.09 µm. These are sampled-window medians. The main seasonal cloud-top calculation counts all droplets that reached their individual critical wet sizes. [July cohort figure data](../../../../website/public/study-data/bai-limonene/july-cohorts.csv)

At three July hours we also saved the evolving temperature, pressure and humidity from each coupled parcel. Replaying dark-aged and sun-aged seeds through both recorded histories held the growth environment fixed in one comparison and the starting particle state fixed in the other. For cohorts present in both outcomes, switching the cloud history added 1.13 µm to median wet diameter in the clean, slow ascent. Switching the aged seed state at a fixed history contributed −0.04 µm. The paired histories identify greater water availability during ascent as the reason the surviving droplets grew larger.

## Could a cloud probe resolve the predicted size shift?

We applied synthetic diameter shifts to a measured liquid-cloud spectrum from Pallas. Two probes sampled the same 99 minutes on 25 October 2015. The forward-scattering spectrometer’s native spectrum gave 132 droplets/cm³ and a number-median wet diameter of 8.38 µm. Even after restricting both probes to their common 3.5–40 µm window, their event medians differed by 2.68 µm. Applying a +1.20 µm shift and rebinning the spectra recovered a positive paired difference in all nine five-minute block pairs on each instrument. A +0.30 µm shift gave the correct sign in six of nine pairs on the forward-scattering probe and eight of nine on the cloud and aerosol spectrometer. The event supplies a measurement scale for a future experiment. Its droplets experienced different source and cloud conditions from the model. [Doulgeris et al., data and instrument methods](https://essd.copernicus.org/articles/14/637/2022/) · [PaCE 2015 archive](https://fmi.b2share.csc.fi/records/va6sm-a6894)

The size bins also interact with the shape of the particular cloud. Most simulated clean, slow July cloud-top droplets fell within one 20–25 µm channel of the cloud and aerosol spectrometer. A synthetic instrument using those edges recovered less than 0.01 µm of the modeled shift. The forward-scattering probe’s bins recovered +1.22 µm. Sampling the full cloud depth broadened the size spectrum. The two instruments then recovered +0.92 and +0.94 µm, respectively. A paired field test needs a common probe, sampling height and size window, with simultaneous measurements of the incoming aerosol and updraft.

## How stable are the simulated differences?

The RTX 5060 calculations used an hourly grid of 162 weighted dry particle sizes. Eleven of 9,460 CPU comparisons exceeded an initial tolerance of 0.00001 droplets/cm³ or 0.000001 µm in median diameter at the original 0.05 s time step. All eleven were clean, strong June updraft cases near the activation threshold. The largest count difference was 0.20 droplets/cm³. Replaying those cases with a 0.0125 s step reduced the maximum CPU–GPU count difference below 0.000001 droplets/cm³. Agreement on a shared grid tests the implementations. A separate refinement tests whether the dry-size grid resolves the activation threshold itself.

The seasonal estimates used 1,728 weighted dry sizes, with cloud states every three hours in July and every six in other weeks. Irradiance still changed every hour. At a bright July hour, doubling the dry-size resolution and halving the time step changed the paired reflected flux by at most 0.06 W/m² across the four parcel regimes. The coarse size grid could change the ordering of the two clean updrafts and even the sign of individual weak winter count responses. Across 16 matched month-and-regime checks, thinning hourly cloud states to six-hour spacing changed weekly mean flux by at most 0.0085 W/m², although an individual interpolated hour differed by as much as 0.414 W/m².

Weather introduces a larger spread. The daily mean reflected change in the clean, slowly rising summer parcel ranged from −2.85 to −0.55 W/m². Omitting each sampled summer month in turn gave −1.58 to −1.33 W/m², around the three-week mean of −1.45 W/m². Those estimates hold the cloud depth and source population fixed. A regional estimate would also need the frequency of suitable liquid clouds, the organic emissions supplying them, and the source-to-cloud travel times.
