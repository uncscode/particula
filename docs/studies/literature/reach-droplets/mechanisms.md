# A burst of simulated mergers matches the fan-off peak but misses its rise

A concentration peak means that, on average, more large droplets occupied each cubic centimetre sampled by the camera during that interval. It leaves open how those droplets reached that size and location. In the fan-off expansion, a model that accelerated collisions between middle-sized parents produced **0.381 droplets cm⁻³ at 50–65 µm** during 5–5.25 s, close to the observed 0.415 cm⁻³. By the time the measured concentration rose, the modeled population was already declining. Fitting the peak itself allowed that merger pulse. When the surrounding spectra alone constrained the collision and transport histories, the predicted burst was much weaker. The rise and fall of both parent and product sizes put stronger limits on the explanation than the peak height alone. [Sagan et al., Figures 6–7](https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0344133) · [Measured and predicted histories](index.md)

## Measured gas forcing leaves too few large droplets in the forward replay

The forward calculation started once from each expansion’s visible 2.5–3 s spectrum. Each pure-water droplet condensed or evaporated into a shared, finite vapor reservoir between updates from the vapor measurement. Its changing diameter affected wall deposition and the probability of colliding with another drop. Brownian motion, differential settling and turbulent shear contributed to the physical collision kernel, the rate coefficient for encounters between two sizes. After initialization the calculation used no further droplet counts. [Water, collision and deposition model](methods.md)

Over 4–7 s the fan-on and fan-off large-tail predictions were 0.122 and 0.085 cm⁻³, compared with 0.260 and 0.183 cm⁻³ measured. The model recovered the ratio between fan settings while underpredicting both absolute concentrations. Switching off turbulent shear in a paired fan-on calculation changed the tail by only 0.00003 cm⁻³. In the full refined fan-on simulation, 18 droplet pairs merged across 24,576 initially simulated droplets, and 4,979 droplets deposited. These controls limit the contribution of the specified collision kernels over this short interval. Local clustering, relative velocities and water exposure along individual trajectories were unmeasured. [Physical process comparisons](data.md)

## Transport can enrich the camera view without producing new large droplets

We next represented the chamber as a cylinder containing connected volumes. Air carried vapor and droplets along a prescribed circulation, and intercell diffusion mixed neighboring volumes. Droplets also settled, exchanged water and deposited. The initial radial concentration away from the camera was inferred. The temperature probes, near the top of the chamber, and the separate vapor beam constrain parts of the gas history. They leave the three-dimensional flow and local vapor field open. [Experimental geometry and calibration](camera.md) · [Spatial assumptions](methods.md)

With standard collisions, reversing the circulation at 1.5 times the reference speed and suppressing intercell diffusion gave a 5–7 s mean of **0.205 cm⁻³** in the nominal camera and **0.047 cm⁻³** chamber-wide. In the 4,608-cell size-bin model, this flow brought enough existing large droplets into view to fall within the measured 5–7 s range of 0.165–0.214 cm⁻³. It produced only 0.213 cm⁻³ during 5–5.25 s, against 0.415 measured. A particle-resolved replay of the same flow gave 0.164 cm⁻³ for the mean and 0.163 cm⁻³ for the quarter. The apparent agreement depended on numerical representation as well as the assumed transport. [Sectional and particle-resolved comparisons](data.md)

## A fitted collision pulse produces the right count at the wrong time

To isolate local production, we held circulation and mixing at their reference values. From 3 to 5 s we multiplied the collision rate by 15,000 whenever both parents were 20–48 µm in diameter. Each merger conserves liquid water and converts two parent drops into one larger drop. The multiplier was selected using the observed fan-off burst. Its magnitude describes the rate the model needed. The experiment supplied no measurement of an enhancement of this size.

The merger simulation reached its maximum at 4.325 s, while the measured burst peaked in the 5–5.25 s quarter. Its predicted concentration fell within the optical block-resampling range in only two of eight late quarters. The time-averaged central signal also obscured a large spatial difference. Over 5–7 s, the particle calculation with the merger pulse gave 0.173 cm⁻³ chamber-wide. The reversed-flow calculation with standard collisions gave 0.039 cm⁻³. Only the concentration in the camera volume was measured.

## Withholding the burst tests what the rest of the spectrum can predict

A separate comparison withheld 5–5.5 s and fitted the full distribution on either side, using 3–5 s and 5.5–6.25 s. This tests interpolation across the burst. After 6.25 s the same models ran forward under measured gas forcing. If the surrounding distributions were sufficient to fix a merger pulse or an influx of already-large droplets, the fitted controls would recover the missing large-droplet count.

The blocked particle fit gave **0.117 cm⁻³** at 50–65 µm during the fan-off gap against **0.398 cm⁻³** measured. A size-bin calculation that used earlier camera residuals to relocate existing droplets within the chamber reached 0.172 cm⁻³. Each relocation conserved the number of droplets in its size class throughout the chamber, and updates stopped inside the gap. Allowing an additional unobserved population below 10 µm instead raised the predicted 11–20 µm concentration to 0.181 cm⁻³ against 0.140 measured, while the large tail fell to 0.087 cm⁻³. Supplying small drops repaired one part of the distribution while widening the large-tail deficit. All comparisons share the same two source expansions. [Fits, scoring windows and held-out spectra](index.md) · [Supporting numerical results](data.md)

A stronger test follows both sizes and trajectories. An arriving parcel can increase the tail at a second height before it reaches the original beam. Local mergers should also deplete the parent sizes, while condensation can shift neighboring size classes together under favorable local vapor conditions. Synchronized size and velocity measurements in repeated expansions could distinguish these signals even when two models give the same central peak. [Two-camera predictions](camera.md)
