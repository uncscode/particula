      At 248 K, similar CLOUD formation rates admit different chemical pathways

      Studies / Literature analyses

      At 248.3 K and 0.38 pptv ammonia, Dunne et al.’s CLOUD formation rates leave open how many particles formed through sulfuric acid and water alone. The model allowing this pathway missed four withheld rates by a typical factor of 3.17. The model requiring ammonia missed them by a factor of 3.04. In a second test, we fixed the ammonia-independent share at one of those conditions (1.80 × 10⁷ sulfuric acid molecules cm⁻³) and refitted the other neutral measurements. Shares of 0.01%, 50% and 99.99% gave median training error factors of 2.64, 2.62 and 2.70, respectively. Three companion dimer measurements at higher ammonia tested a different part of the chemistry and favored Kürten’s optimized cluster evaporation parameters. In a separate warm CLOUD run, the acid history reconstructed from OH + SO₂ and scaled to the early measured mean improved predicted counts above 9.7 nm during the peak in 511 of 512 paired simulations, yet the median peak remained 24% below the measurement. Together these results motivate measuring cluster composition, formation rate and size-resolved growth during the same run. Formation-rate overview (CSV)

      What Dunne and CLOUD measured at 1.7 nm

      Section titled “What Dunne and CLOUD measured at 1.7 nm”

      Dunne et al. measured inorganic particle formation across chamber temperatures, sulfuric acid and ammonia concentrations, and ion production rates. Their atmospheric model combined neutral and ion-induced contributions with and without ammonia. Removing the neutral binary term and refitting the remaining channels still explained much of the observed variation. Of 377 archived chamber records, 267 fall within 33–43% RH, including 82 neutral observations and seven neutral observations below 1 pptv ammonia. At the coldest temperatures the authors inferred the background ammonia and described uncertainties reaching a factor of ten. Dunne et al., 2016 · Original data · Dunne et al., supplementary materials

      J₁.₇ is the number of particles crossing 1.7 nm mobility diameter per cubic centimetre per second. CLOUD usually inferred it from a counter with a 3.2 nm cutoff, correcting for growth, dilution, coagulation and wall loss. The authors estimated an overall factor-of-2.5 systematic rate uncertainty. A simulation that propagates the published rate back to 3.2 nm consequently shares some of the assumptions used to derive it. The cold archive lacks simultaneous counter histories keyed to individual rows. The warm comparison uses another campaign with measured size distributions and very different ammonia and temperature. Counter correction · Kürten et al., chamber instrumentation

      We use Particula to ask which chemical pathways can explain the cold formation rates and how many newly formed particles survive to a detectable size. We test competing descriptions of ammonia’s role against withheld CLOUD rates and compare predicted neutral dimer concentrations with companion measurements. We then follow particles from 1.7 to 3.2 and 10 nm as they grow, coagulate and deposit on chamber walls. In the warm run, we test whether sulfur chemistry and water uptake can account for the measured rise in particle counts above 9.7 nm. Joint Monte Carlo simulations show which conclusions persist as gas calibrations and model parameters vary, and guide our choice of measurements for a 223 K repeat.

      What changes when the uncertainties vary together?

      Section titled “What changes when the uncertainties vary together?”

      For the cold study we used 128 sets of jointly varied parameters, in four independent blocks. Each draw shares calibration shifts across affected observations, refits the model after removing the entire designated holdout, and supplies the corresponding gases and source to the downstream simulations. We used separate calibrations for the companion experiments. The warm ensemble pairs 16 model variants at each of 512 combinations of gas, wall, coagulation and boundary conditions. Throughout, 5th–95th percentile bands describe the range of simulations under the stated inputs. Statistical confidence in a chemical pathway would require an observational error model and information about parameter correlations that these archives do not provide.

      The cold draws used four independently scrambled Sobol sequences of 32 draws each. Within each draw we varied sulfuric acid by a factor of up to 1.5 in either direction and corrected formation rates by up to 2.5. For ammonia we used factors of 10 below 1 pptv, 2 at 1–10 pptv, 1.5 above 10 and below 100 pptv, and 1.2 at 100 pptv and above. We sampled these multipliers uniformly in log space and shared them across rows in each concentration bracket. We also varied the weight of each training condition and used the same weights for both competing models. The choice of brackets and their correlations affects the width of the reported simulation ranges. Dunne et al., supplementary materials, §§6–8 and 19

      Dimer measurements distinguish two thermochemical parameter sets

      Section titled “Dimer measurements distinguish two thermochemical parameter sets”

      At three 248 K CLOUD conditions, neutral dimer measurements let us compare two fixed sets of evaporation parameters within the same cluster model. One set comes from Kürten’s optimization. The other uses the published marginal median for each parameter. For each thermochemical parameter set, we calculated how often neutral molecules and clusters collide using two versions of Particula’s collision kernel. The first treats them as hard spheres (Hamaker constant 0 J). The second includes van der Waals attraction (Hamaker constant 6.4 × 10⁻²⁰ J). With attraction included, the median modeled concentrations using Kürten’s optimized thermochemistry were 0.85, 0.96 and 0.57 times the measured values at approximately 7.3, 4.4 and 2.3 pptv ammonia. Combining the three measurements, this gives a median error factor of 1.50, against 6.25 using the published marginal median parameters. Under each collision kernel, the optimized set gave the smaller combined error in all 128 paired draws. Each draw reused the same three observations while varying the assumed gas concentrations, instrument calibration and chamber losses. At 265 K in an independent flow tube, the optimized model gave only 0.46 times the reported dimer abundance, with a 5th–95th percentile range of 0.27–0.78 under an assumed factor-of-1.5 gas range. Transfer to that higher-concentration experiment remains incomplete. Kürten et al., Fig. 9 and Table 3 · Hanson and Eisele, flow-tube measurements

      The cold growth law changes how much of the 1.7 nm source reaches a counter

      Section titled “The cold growth law changes how much of the 1.7 nm source reaches a counter”

      Treating sulfuric acid as a finite molecule attracted to the growing particle increased modeled 10 nm survival in all 768 paired comparisons across the six cold holdouts. At 248.3 K and 1.80 × 10⁷ acid molecules cm⁻³ with 0.38 pptv ammonia, median survival was 0.000478% for vapor transfer and 0.205% with finite molecular size and attraction. The median within-draw gain was 449-fold. Across the 128 joint draws, the central 90% of survival probabilities spanned 2.1 × 10⁻¹¹ to 1.43 × 10⁻³ for vapor transfer and 5.85 × 10⁻⁶ to 3.46 × 10⁻² for attraction. Both calculations started with the same seed composition under the same sampled acid, ammonia, wall deposition, hydration, sticking and background aerosol. We used Particula’s size-dependent spherical-chamber deposition in every draw, anchored at 3 nm to the historical cold chamber approximation. Cold wall and growth histories have no row-specific calibration. For this condition, the 5th percentile for vapor transfer varied from 4.5 × 10⁻¹³ to 7.2 × 10⁻¹⁰ across four independent 32-draw blocks.

      What published follow-on work adds to the Particula study

      Section titled “What published follow-on work adds to the Particula study”

      Which molecules stabilized the cold clusters? Kürten (2019) used 125 neutral CLOUD formation rates to infer how readily small sulfuric acid–ammonia clusters evaporate. Yu et al. (2020) compared mechanistic rate tables with all 377 Dunne observations, classifying conditions below 0.1 pptv ammonia as binary. That rule places both of our 223 K measurements in the binary group. Our refits ask whether their measured rates can distinguish the pathways when the ammonia background is uncertain. Because these papers draw on overlapping CLOUD records, the most informative additional comparison here is the measured abundance of neutral dimers. At three higher-ammonia companion conditions, the dimers favored Kürten’s optimized evaporation parameters over the set assembled from marginal medians. This tests the predicted cluster abundance. The binary share at the original low-ammonia condition remains open. Kürten (2019), Table 1 and Figs. 3–4 · Yu et al. (2020), §3

      What determines whether a particle reaches a counter? Stolzenburg et al. (2020) measured how quickly uncharged sulfuric acid particles grew from 1.8 to 10 nm in CLOUD. At 278 and 293 K, the smallest particles grew faster than a hard-sphere collision model predicted, consistent with van der Waals attraction. That finding motivates our attractive-capture calculation. In Particula, combining finite molecular size and attraction gave a median 449-fold increase in 10 nm survival across paired simulations relative to vapor transfer at 248.3 K and 0.38 pptv ammonia. That survival estimate extrapolates beyond the temperatures and ammonia concentrations they measured. Marten et al. (2022) showed how to test survival experimentally. They compared formation rates at 6 and 2.5 nm while varying the loss to pre-existing aerosol. Rapid ammonium nitrate growth helped particles survive in their haze experiments. Their two-threshold measurement offers a design for testing our cold replay under Dunne’s sulfuric acid conditions. Stolzenburg et al. (2020), Figs. 1 and 4 · Marten et al. (2022), Fig. 3

      How much of the warm growth can sulfur chemistry explain? Donahue et al. (2026) published the acid and size-distribution histories from CLOUD run 1833, conducted in 2016 to calibrate the chamber lights. They used particle growth to constrain the absolute sulfuric acid calibration. Our Particula replay uses those same spectra to supply particles at 2.9 nm and evaluate counts above 9.7 nm. After scaling the reconstructed acid history to the early measured mean, it improved peak-window counts in 511 of 512 paired simulations. Both the acid exposure during the peak and the modeled particle influx at 2.9 nm can change in this comparison. The shared growth calibration and particle measurements limit what that agreement can establish about an additional sulfur reaction. Donahue et al. (2026), Figs. 6 and 10

      Why does survival matter beyond the chamber? Gordon et al. (2017) carried Dunne’s inorganic formation rates into a global aerosol model alongside other formation pathways. They estimated that new particle formation from all pathways supplied 54% of present-day cloud condensation nuclei at low-cloud altitude and 0.2% supersaturation. Our analysis examines how many newly formed particles survive their first few nanometres of growth. Extending the chamber results to cloud condensation nuclei would also require atmospheric vapour histories, aerosol losses and growth to activation size. Gordon et al. (2017)

      Conclusions

      Section titled “Conclusions”

      Cold formation. At 248.3 K and 0.38 pptv ammonia, two models gave roughly threefold errors on the four withheld formation rates. One model required ammonia for every particle. The other allowed sulfuric acid and water to form particles on their own. The total rate therefore leaves the contribution of each pathway unresolved. The dimer measurements constrain a different quantity. At three higher-ammonia companion conditions, Kürten’s optimized evaporation parameters predicted dimer abundance more closely than the marginal-median set in all 128 paired comparisons under each collision model. Measuring dimers at 0.38 pptv ammonia would test whether that result carries over to the original cold conditions.

      Growth after formation. In the cold simulations, including both the finite size of a sulfuric acid molecule and its attraction to a particle increased survival to 10 nm relative to vapor transfer. Testing that prediction requires size-resolved counts during the same cold run. The warm CLOUD experiment supplied such a size distribution. In that run, the OH + SO₂ acid history scaled to the first 3.8 h of measurements reduced the subsequent peak-window error in 511 of 512 simulations. The improvement includes changes in acid exposure and in particle entry at 2.9 nm. The median predicted peak above 9.7 nm was still about 24% below the measured peak. Additional sulfur chemistry could bring the peak closer, but the later counts did not consistently favor it. A sulfur tracer measured alongside gas-phase acid and particle sulfate would test that explanation.

      Priority experiment. Repeat the two 223 K acid settings in one controlled scan, with intermediate acid steps, fixed relative humidity, and an independent measurement of the ammonia background. Measure the size-resolved growth and loss at each step to bound their relative effect on the inferred formation-rate ratio. The archived rates differ by a factor of 49 as sulfuric acid nearly doubles. Models fitted without those two observations predict median increases of about 9-fold with the binary pathway allowed and 1.2-fold when ammonia is required. Their simulation ranges overlap, so a modest increase could fit either explanation. A reproducible 49-fold increase with a tightly constrained relative correction would challenge both models’ central prediction ranges. Measuring neutral monomers and dimers during the scan would show how cluster abundance changes alongside the formation rate.
