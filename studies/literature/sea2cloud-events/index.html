      Sea2Cloud’s March 22 spectra favor earlier arrival of larger particles in a two-hour exchange model

      Studies / Literature analyses

      On March 22 the Tangaroa encountered a burst of 1–10 nm particles while the ship’s 12–25 nm count was already falling. Could the same incoming pulse explain both populations? In a box exchanging air over two hours, we fitted the small-particle pulse and used only the 06:00 UTC larger-size spectrum to set the amplitude of an incoming 16 nm component. At 06:30 the ship recorded 28 particles cm⁻³ at 12–25 nm. Simultaneous arrival predicts 300 cm⁻³. Moving the larger particles one hour earlier predicts 36 cm⁻³. One- and two-hour leads fit the seven later spectra almost equally well. The comparison favors an arrival sequence under these exchange assumptions. The shipboard record leaves the precise lag and upwind origin unresolved. Salignat et al., Figures 3b and 5a · March 22 fit and withheld spectra

      What the regional model and the ship can each tell us

      Section titled “What the regional model and the ship can each tell us”

      Salignat et al. studied nighttime particle formation above the biologically active Chatham Rise off New Zealand. They used a marine detection criterion of 500 particles cm⁻³ at 1–10 nm. This criterion can identify a burst without requiring the sustained growth to larger sizes used to recognize many continental events. In their WRF-Chem simulations, a chamber-derived formation law using nitric acid as a proxy for nitrate ions reproduced the March 19 event better than the default sulfuric-acid schemes. The March 22 event remained unexplained. The study is a preprint. Paper, sections 3.1.3 and 3.2 · Marine detection criterion

      The regional model follows formation, growth and transport before air reaches the vessel. Particula lets us ask a narrower question. Given the modeled acid at the ship, how many particles could form there, how many would have to enter with the air, and where should we find them in the size spectrum afterward? We use the published, exhaust-filtered Figure 5a for the ambient 1–10 nm target. Its half-hour spans enclose visible observations. Their midpoints are fitting targets, and their upper edges retain brief peaks. The estimated plot-reading precision is about 30 cm⁻³. For the independent larger-size comparison, we integrate the digitized Figure 3b distribution in log diameter. The plotted concentrations already include the authors’ SMPS correction. Paper, Figures 3 and 5 · Acid and trajectory data, Figures S3 and S5

      A changing inflow can supply a peak. Can its arrival be predicted?

      Section titled “A changing inflow can supply a peak. Can its arrival be predicted?”

      We replace air continuously in a well-mixed box. For each size class, exchange contributes the difference between its incoming and resident number concentrations divided by the exchange time. Particles also grow by organic condensation and are scavenged by the measured background. The central test uses a two-hour exchange time, 80% relative humidity and 10⁶ condensable molecules cm⁻³. Entering small particles are divided equally among four dry diameters (1.5, 3, 6 and 8 nm). We initially give each incoming population the same pre-event 12–25 nm background, then test an explicit change to that assumption.

      The vessel moved only 23 and 26 km between the endpoints of the respective 12-hour pre-event tracks. Air at the reported median wind speed would travel about 449 km in that time. The published trajectories indicate 14–18 hours of preceding marine boundary-layer residence on March 19 and 12–24 hours on March 22. We found no trajectory-matched upwind size distribution for either arrival. The Baring Head 2020 aerosol campaign began after the voyage. We therefore test a shipboard indicator and an event-conditioned inflow, each with an explicit limit on what it can establish. Ship positions · Back trajectories · Baring Head sampling periods

      The replay crossed 500 cm⁻³ near the March 19 onset, then peaked around 13:45 UTC, about 90 minutes before the highest observed bin. Its average distance outside the March 19 observed ranges was 271 cm⁻³, compared with 149 cm⁻³ for constant inflow. A better peak magnitude alone would have given the wrong impression about timing. On March 22 the replay stayed below 500 throughout. The event-conditioned pulse crossed the threshold around 06:21 UTC. At the 06:45 peak-bin center it gave 673 cm⁻³, or 592 cm⁻³ when that bin was withheld. Both fits use the subsequent decay. Their timing is a retrospective inference.

      With two-hour exchange, the fitted incoming air reaches about 4,060 cm⁻³ on March 19 and 3,280 cm⁻³ on March 22. Both fits choose the shortest tested pulse, 30 minutes wide at half maximum. The exchange model smooths that pulse before it reaches the modeled counter. These concentrations describe what this box requires under its assumed mixing time. The voyage provides no upwind measurement confirming either amplitude or width.

      Shading marks the reported events. The 25–100 nm ship indicator supplies a history independent of the target size bands. The pulse curve uses the event itself.       The March 22 size spectrum distinguishes histories with similar peaks

      Section titled “The March 22 size spectrum distinguishes histories with similar peaks”

      With only sub-10 nm particles in the pulse, the central calculation gives 0.84–1.05 cm⁻³ at 12–25 nm over the March 22 comparison. Adding a simultaneous 16 nm component at 5% gives 5.8 cm⁻³ at 06:00 and 34.4 at 06:30. The ship measured 45.1 and 27.9, respectively.       Local production leaves a measurable number deficit

      Section titled “Local production leaves a measurable number deficit”

      At the chamber calibration ceiling of 2 × 10⁷ HNO₃ molecules cm⁻³, the published law produces about 157 particles cm⁻³ h⁻¹. Even seven hours at that ceiling with perfect survival supplies only 1,100 cm⁻³ on March 19. One hour supplies 157 cm⁻³ on March 22. Using the recovered ship-track acid gives much lower local peaks of 2.24 and 0.021 cm⁻³ in the original 12-hour-history calculation. March 19 lacks acid for its first three initialization hours. Holding that interval at the calibration ceiling gives a maximum of 403 cm⁻³ in the slow-growth, weak-scavenging case, at the beginning of the event. Runnable production calculation · Pre-event hindcasts

      The observed rise constrains how much additional number the ship must encounter. We subtract the highest earlier count, allow 30 cm⁻³ of reading error at each endpoint, and subtract an upper estimate of all local births between the outer edges of the two bins. The strongest resulting bounds are 997 cm⁻³ within two hours on March 19 (13:45 to 15:15 bin centers) and 607 cm⁻³ within 12.5 hours on March 22 (18:45 the preceding day to 06:45). Scavenging and growth past 10 nm increase the required supply. Changing air masses and formation from another precursor could both contribute. These bounds assume comparable ambient sampling and growth toward larger diameters. Number budgets and bin times.

      Growth can remove small particles and still put them in the wrong size band

      Section titled “Growth can remove small particles and still put them in the wrong size band”

      The earlier CUDA optimization fitted organic uptake, initial inventory and a continuous source to pre-event small-particle counts. On March 19, growth improved the pre-event midpoint RMSE from 594 to 581 cm⁻³. Yet that fit placed 245–695 cm⁻³ at 12–25 nm, compared with the measured 0.37–8.28. Across 19 larger-size observations the mean absolute error was 489 cm⁻³, falling to 1.98 cm⁻³ with organic uptake switched off.       Follow the same incoming air to test particle origin

      Section titled “Follow the same incoming air to test particle origin”

      Local ship-track nitric acid cannot supply the observed number fast enough under the published formation law. The regional nitrate mechanism can still contribute upwind, where residence time, concentrations and transport differ. March 19 illustrates why both scale and timing matter: replaying earlier variability recovers a threshold crossing, yet places the peak early. Assigning the decline entirely to rapid growth creates a larger-particle population far in excess of the measured spectrum. These two independent tests narrow the possible histories in a way that matching the sub-10 nm maximum alone cannot.

      On March 22, incoming particles can supply much of the observed number even with little local acid-driven production. The rapidly declining 12–25 nm population was present by the start of the event. Within the tested exchange model, its later evolution is better reproduced when the larger-particle enhancement precedes the smallest particles. In the two-hour exchange model, one- and two-hour leads give almost identical mean errors on the seven later spectra. With six-hour exchange and the highest tested vapor level, even simultaneous arrival fits about as well. The timing comparison constrains the ordering within the central model and leaves the transport time and chemical origin open.

      The most discriminating next comparison would follow the same incoming air with synchronized 1–3, 3–10 and 12–25 nm counts, beginning before the larger-size enhancement. Co-located nitric acid or nitrate-ion measurements would test formation along that path. At the ship, verified valve times, counter response and the exhaust mask would allow the 1–3 nm channel to constrain the source size: earlier fits supplying particles at 1 or 5 nm predicted almost identical March 22 1–10 nm peaks of 186–187 cm⁻³, yet predicted 73.7 versus 0.018 cm⁻³ at 1–3 nm. Until those inputs are available, the particle budgets and the ordering of the size changes provide firmer conclusions than a choice of precursor. Precursor hypotheses and campaign measurements

      Methods and reproducible results

      Section titled “Methods and reproducible results”
      • Exchange and boundary fits. We initialize March 19 at 21:00 UTC on March 18 and March 22 at 06:00 UTC on March 21. The first three hours use zero missing acid in the exchange study. Units are hours, dry diameter in nm and particles cm⁻³. Survival along each size trajectory includes Particula Brownian scavenging and exponential replacement at rate 1/τ. Inflow adds the external concentration divided by τ. The local nucleation source uses the interpolated hourly acid. Background and incoming concentrations apply to a stationary box on the moving ship track.
      • Incoming history. Pre-event data set the initial inventory and either one constant concentration or two concentrations interpolated against the 25–100 nm indicator. Its pre-event 10th and 90th percentiles define the two ends. Each spectrum becomes available at its half-hour bin end. Gaps beyond two hours use the pre-event median. Replay repeats the previous 12 hours of this indicator. The concurrent case updates it with later ship spectra. No post-onset 1–10 nm measurements enter either fit. Every coefficient lies between zero and 6,000 cm⁻³.
      • Pulse and uncertainty. A steady reservoir plus one Gaussian pulse is fitted independently to each event and six post-event hours, holding the initial inventory from the constant pre-event fit. The full fit targets the observed upper edge in the peak bin and midpoints elsewhere. The withheld fit omits that bin entirely. Pulse centers span three hours before onset through the event end in 15-minute increments. Widths at half maximum span 0.5, 1, 2 and 4 hours. We vary τ over 0.5, 2 and 6 hours and vapor over 0, 1 and 4 × 10⁶ molecules cm⁻³. Fitted event maxima span 1,025–1,418 and 473–765 cm⁻³ across those inputs. These scenario ranges describe model sensitivity. Fixed 5% additions affect only the pulse excess. The size-lead test fits a separate nonnegative 16 nm amplitude to the first larger-size observation, then freezes it.
      • Growth and detection. The box uses 287.35 K, 101325 Pa, dry density 1500 kg m⁻³, κ = 0.3, curvature-corrected water uptake and a fixed pre-event median scavenging spectrum. The organic tracer has molar mass 200 g mol⁻¹, diffusivity 6 × 10⁻⁶ m² s⁻¹, unit accommodation and negligible equilibrium vapor pressure. Water and vapor come from external reservoirs. Small-particle self-coagulation, composition-dependent evaporation and a trajectory-specific mixing rate remain unmodeled. Smooth upper cuts have a width of 5% of nominal dry diameter. The ship’s instrument response and losses near the SMPS lower cutoff introduce additional uncertainty.
      • Counters. The 1–3 nm estimate differences alternating PSM saturator settings. We discard settling periods, require stable plateaus, interpolate the adjacent low-flow readings and match the independent CPC within 30 s. The candidate inlet uses three successive plateaus in the same VOC-tracer window. The CPC archive names a TSI 3772 and the paper names a 3010. The main-inlet SMPS deposit describes 64 channels, but its linked file has the 26 channels and cadence of the sea-spray generator. Figure 3b supplies our larger-size reference. Main-inlet deposit · Sea-spray deposit
      • Numerics. For the exchange study, the CPU builds Particula collision and growth coefficients, propagates cohorts by convolution and fits the boundary concentrations. Independent direct cohort sums on an NVIDIA RTX 5060 agree on the central sub-10 nm inflow contribution to better than 10⁻⁸ cm⁻³. Halving the three-minute step changes central band counts by less than 0.03 cm⁻³. A lossless unit reservoir stays within 0.00042 of equilibrium. In the preceding CUDA inverse study, all four automatic derivatives of the fit objective agree with finite differences within 10⁻⁶ relative error.
