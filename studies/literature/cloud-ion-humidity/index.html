      At low ion production, CLOUD’s 70% humidity measurement exceeds the ceiling set by the reported wall loss

      Studies / Literature analyses

      At 70% relative humidity and 20 °C, Franchin et al. measured 1,768 ions cm⁻³ per polarity in the CERN CLOUD chamber at a nominal source of 2.64 ion pairs cm⁻³ s⁻¹. Could slower recombination account for so many ions? Even switching recombination off leaves a well-mixed steady population of at most 394 ions cm⁻³ when the lower end of the wall-loss rate reported near 38% humidity is applied to the humid run. The measured scatter bar reached down to 1,579 ions cm⁻³, four times that ceiling. At high production, humidity-dependent recombination accounts for about half the measured dry-to-humid rise. The low-production result gives a sharper test. With that source and loss, tuning recombination cannot recover the humid count. Independently clearing the humid sampling path would test whether a reading persists when the physical ion concentration is near zero. Franchin et al., Figure 5d and Table A2 · Digitized steady readings and scatter

      How CLOUD measured humidity-dependent ion recombination

      Section titled “How CLOUD measured humidity-dependent ion recombination”

      Franchin and colleagues varied ion production with a particle beam in a 26.1 m³ chamber. Their spectrometer sampled positive and negative cluster ions. Figure 5d gives the mean concentration of the two signs, averaged over the final 10–15 minutes of each setting. The dry and humid measurements came from separate runs. The authors used these concentrations to estimate how the recombination coefficient varied with humidity. Reusing those coefficients tests how well their fitted rates and a common first-order loss can account for the observed pattern across beam settings. Experimental method and Figure 5d

      A verified humid zero would distinguish an offset from a source

      Section titled “A verified humid zero would distinguish an offset from a source”

      Both fitted models use cm³ s⁻¹. The useful intervention is to establish the physical zero independently through the humid sampling path. Repeat the 2.28 and 2.64 ion-pair settings, switch off the beam, hold the clearing field for 120 s, then release to a background of 1.84 ion pairs cm⁻³ s⁻¹. Record both polarities at 5, 10, 30, 60 and 120 s. With a starting reading of 1,407 ions cm⁻³, residual concentrations from 0–100 ions cm⁻³, detector time constants from 0–60 s and unit gain, the predicted reading 30 s after release is 1,086–1,196 for the offset and 82–286 ions cm⁻³ for the extra source. The ranges describe synthetic trajectories.

      In a blinded recovery test, one inlet distinguished these two fitted cases in 512 of 512 trials in each direction (pointwise 95% Wilson lower bound 99.3%). Separating localized production from nonuniform wall loss also requires a spatial measurement. With the reference noise and inlet gains known to ±5%, the second inlet needed to sample at most half the plume fraction of the first. At ±10% gain the smallest passing reduction tested rose to 62.5%, and at ±15% to 87.5%. At double the noise, only exactly known gains and complete exposure contrast passed. These thresholds apply to the four fitted hypotheses and tested grid. Co-locate the counters to measure humid gain and noise, then independently establish the contrast with a beam profile and a transport or tracer measurement.

      The low-source counts rule out recovering the humid signal by adjusting recombination alone within the shared-loss model. A persistently high reading after independently clearing the humid sampling path would support an offset. A reading near the physical zero followed by a rise would favor renewed production in these fitted cases. Explaining where that production occurs would still require the two calibrated inlets and a measured exposure contrast.
