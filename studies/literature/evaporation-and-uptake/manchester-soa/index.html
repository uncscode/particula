      How well does Particula reproduce organic aerosol formation in the Manchester chamber?

      Studies / Literature analyses / Evaporation and reactive uptake

      How well can a model reproduce both the amount of aerosol a chamber produces and the sizes of its particles? We used Particula to search for chemistry shared across 15 Manchester chamber experiments. For the mixture of α-pinene, o-cresol and isoprene, Voliotis and colleagues measured a wall-corrected organic aerosol yield of 11.7% at 461 µg/m³ of total precursor consumed. Their single-precursor fits gave 16.1% when evaluated at the modeled consumption of each precursor. A Particula bulk model fitted to mass and particle number gave 13.8%. Particle size offered a second test. At two hours, a particle-resolved model using chemistry from the bulk mass fit placed the peak one diameter bin above the measured peak. Including size measurements in the refit brought the peak into the measured 126–158 nm bin, although the refit underestimated the five-hour geometric mean diameter. Across the campaign, several reaction pathways could reproduce similar mass histories, leaving their chemical products unresolved.

      What Manchester’s precursor mixtures revealed

      Section titled “What Manchester’s precursor mixtures revealed”

      Voliotis and colleagues asked whether yields measured for individual precursors could explain the aerosol produced by their mixtures. They illuminated α-pinene, o-cresol and isoprene with nitrogen oxides and ammonium sulfate seed in an 18 m³ chamber. At matched precursor consumption, the α-pinene/o-cresol pair produced more SOA than expected from the single-precursor yields. Isoprene suppressed the α-pinene yield. A possible enhancement with o-cresol and isoprene was too small to resolve, and the ternary yield was close to the single-precursor expectation. The authors noted that even at matched consumption, different amounts of aerosol remain available for vapors to partition into. Voliotis et al., Figure 5 and conclusions

      Of 24 experiments, 15 had overlapping precursor decay, suspended organic mass and particle counts. Their first five hours supplied 1,258 mass and 682 number readings. We modeled deposition explicitly when comparing suspended mass and added deposited mass back when comparing the authors’ wall-corrected yields. Voliotis et al., experimental design and Figure 3 · Instrument records, coverage and source DOIs

      Companion measurements also distinguish mechanisms with similar yields. Online gas and particle measurements and offline filter analyses identified o-cresol-derived nitroaromatics in both o-cresol binary mixtures. A formula found only in a mixture leaves its structure and carbon sources unresolved. Thermal-denuder and mass-spectrometer measurements also found more low-volatility material in the ternary aerosol than single-precursor contributions predicted. Du et al., online and offline composition · Shao et al., filter composition · Shao et al., carbon oxidation state · Binary-mixture measurements · Isoprene and ternary-mixture measurements

      Wang and colleagues measured water uptake below and above saturation. In an α-pinene experiment, humidified growth factors fell from 1.65–1.72 to about 1.15 within an hour as organics accumulated. Independent particle-rebound experiments showed strong humidity and composition dependence. We used measured water uptake in the fits below. Composition, volatility and rebound informed the interpretation of the chemistry. Wang et al., water uptake, Figures 3–4 · Wang et al., particle rebound, Figures 1–2

      Fitting shared chemistry to 15 chamber histories

      Section titled “Fitting shared chemistry to 15 chamber histories”

      Two runs with nominally 155 ppb α-pinene illustrate why a single mass discrepancy tells us little about missing chemistry. The first reached 68.47 µg m⁻³ and the second 109.46 µg m⁻³. Applying the first run’s fit to the second run’s measured precursor decay and seed conditions gave 76.88 µg m⁻³, rising to 104.23 µg m⁻³ at the favorable bound of the injection uncertainty. The experiments also used different instruments for their initial counts. Transfer experiment · Injection and wall-loss sensitivity

      For the campaign-wide fit, all 15 runs shared chemical parameters. Each supplied its measured parent-precursor decay and could adjust its injection dose by ±15%. We fitted two objectives separately. One minimized the worst mass-trajectory error across runs. The other minimized the worst error over both mass and number. Mass error was the root-mean-square error (RMSE) divided by the observed run peak, with a 1 µg m⁻³ normalization floor. Count error was normalized by initial particle number. The resulting scores measure reproduction of the fitted experiments. Model and scoring details

      Wall deposition and precursor competition

      Section titled “Wall deposition and precursor competition”

      With the early chemistry and coagulation model, replacing constant deposition with size-dependent wall loss reduced worst joint error from 24.93% to 21.41%. In a sweep of 4,096 chemistry and wall-mixing combinations, chemistry accounted for 86.3% of peak-mass variance in the median experiment and wall mixing for 99.3% of five-hour count variance. Those shares depend on the chosen ranges. A wall coefficient fitted to dark seed decay transferred poorly to the illuminated chamber. Wall and chemistry assessment · Per-experiment variance shares

      How the agentic search arrived at 70 channels

      Section titled “How the agentic search arrived at 70 channels”

      In each of the first 20 rounds, the agent selected a precursor-compatible equation, fitted its effective rate with CUDA simulations, and recorded the proposal before seeking an experimental analogue. Twelve proposals had direct evidence for a corresponding process and eight had broader reaction-family support. For instance, irradiation can deplete particle-bound peroxides in α-pinene-derived SOA. This supports a loss pathway while leaving its fitted rate in Manchester undetermined. In 15 s replays the 20-channel search reduced worst mass error from 15.87% to 12.09% and worst joint error from 18.04% to 17.92%.       The first network could form an α-pinene dimer but pooled its downstream products. A second search tested 40 predeclared sequences spanning five product families. Oxidation, fragmentation and further reaction of released vapor added 50 distinct transfers because some sequences share steps. The full network offered 70 channels whose fitted rates could return to zero. Fifteen sequences had a published reaction-family analogue.       Which reactions and products did Particula track?

      Section titled “Which reactions and products did Particula track?”

      The precursors have known molecular structures. Downstream, the model tracks how much organic material each precursor contributes to gas and particle pools. For example, two α-pinene-derived particle intermediates can form a dimer pool. That material can oxidize and remain suspended, or fragment and release a daughter into the gas. In a partial fragmentation step the reference model sends half the transferred mass to the gas and retains half in particles. Manchester’s bulk mass and number records leave the molecular structures of these daughters unresolved.

      Shao and colleagues tentatively assigned a C₉H₁₄O₄ signal in the α-pinene/isoprene aerosol to pinic acid from its fragmentation pattern. The structure in the figure illustrates that assignment.       What changes when each particle carries its own composition?

      Section titled “What changes when each particle carries its own composition?”

      We repeated all 15 experiments with individual particles carrying sulfate and 19 organic inventories. A particle could acquire vapor, undergo aging reactions, merge with a Brownian collision partner or deposit at a rate that depended on its size. A concentration-dependent source could form 3 nm organic particles. The frozen control used the earlier mass-calibrated chemistry and starting particle parameters.       What the simulations add to the Manchester measurements

      Section titled “What the simulations add to the Manchester measurements”

      The separate-precursor replays support Manchester’s finding of enhanced yield for α-pinene mixed with o-cresol. Two fitted mechanisms reproduce the direction of that effect and disagree about its magnitude. The ternary experiment shows what a size measurement adds. Including the measured spectrum in the particle-level fit reduced the error in particle number across diameter bins over five hours, yet the final geometric mean diameter moved farther from the measured value.

      The offset in run 18 was already present at lights-on. Recovering its original dry-size scans is the first priority. Integrating particle number and surface area over the same measured diameter window would test how much of the discrepancy arose when we reconstructed the seed distribution, before asking any added reaction to explain its evolution.

      For chemistry, the missing observable is the time history of an identified product. In an isolated α-pinene dimer chain we exchanged the oxidation and fragmentation rates of 0.1 and 1 h⁻¹. Predicted dimer inventories differed by up to 94.75 µg m⁻³ across the mass-calibrated runs. Total suspended mass differed by less than 10⁻¹² µg m⁻³, and the particle counts were identical. Measuring a structurally identified dimer and its oxidized daughter through the aerosol peak would test which step was slower under this mechanism. Coincident gas-phase fragments would further constrain how much material left the particles. Rate-swap results

      The follow-up network discovery study tests additional reactions against a fresh fit of the earlier chemistry and checks the selected fit against the measured size distributions.
