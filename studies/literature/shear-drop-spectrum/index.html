      Fitting mean drop mass with a constant collision rate leaves a sixfold shortfall in counts at twice the starting radius

      Studies / Literature analyses

      In a modeled cloud that starts with identical drops, fitting the mean drop mass can miss the size distribution that produced it. At about 133 minutes, size-dependent turbulent shear in Particula gave 610 drops of exactly twice the starting radius per million initial drops. Fitting a constant pair collision rate to the same final mean mass gave 102. The sixfold ratio had a 95% sampling interval of 5.49–6.57. Saffman and Turner originally calculated how turbulence broadens the drop spectrum. Independently integrating their shear equations gave 614 in this mass class, compared with about 675 read from their printed Figure 2. All of these counts are predictions. A chamber test would need enough sampled air to detect the rare large drops. Saffman and Turner, equations 1–3 and Figure 2

      How drop size enters the collision rate

      Section titled “How drop size enters the collision rate”

      Saffman and Turner asked whether shear could broaden an initially uniform drop population enough to help initiate rainfall. In their shear calculation, drops are small compared with the smallest turbulent eddies. Nearby fluid velocities differ across a drop pair, so even equal-sized drops can collide. The authors integrated their collision equations and plotted the predicted abundance of each mass class in Figure 2. Saffman and Turner (1956)

      Their cloud example uses 600 water drops/cm³ and 1.5 g of liquid water/m³. At a liquid density of 1,000 kg/m³, each drop starts with a radius of 8.42 µm and a mass of 2.50 ng. Eight times that mass corresponds to twice the initial radius. The drops follow the turbulent air, and every encounter leads to coalescence. Only turbulent shear acts. We use the paper’s example dissipation rate of 0.01 m²/s³ and air kinematic viscosity of 1.7 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s. Original cloud example, pp. 23–24

      A small sample can miss the large drops

      Section titled “A small sample can miss the large drops”

      At , just 141 of 1,024 simulated 0.427 mL boxes (13.8%) contained an exact eightfold drop. Increasing the volume to 1.71 mL raised the fraction to 363 of 768 (47.3%). The pooled abundances were similar at 595 and 610 per million initial drops. Fitting the constant rate to each volume’s own mean mass gave 100 and 102 per million, respectively. The sixfold difference persisted even though the chance of seeing one of these drops in a single sample changed substantially. Earlier, at , only one of the 768 larger boxes contained a drop in this class.

      The authors also estimated roughly 100 drops/L at two different times and minimum masses. These are cumulative counts. At 90 minutes every drop at or above eight times the initial mass contributes, including drops that have outgrown the exact eightfold class.       What would test this in a cloud chamber?

      Section titled “What would test this in a cloud chamber?”

      At the same final mean mass, the two collision laws predict different numbers of large drops in the idealized cloud. To test that distinction in a chamber, measure the initial size distribution, any large drops already present, turbulent dissipation and the probe’s sampling volume. Fit a constant rate using only the total number of drops. Then compare the withheld count at or above twice the initial radius at 90 minutes with both predictions. Calibrate detection near that radius and repeat across independent samples. Initial size spread, gravitational settling, spatial mixing and imperfect coalescence could all alter the measured tail. The reported intervals describe simulation sampling under the stated assumptions.
