      How strongly does curvature raise equilibrium vapor pressure?

      Section titled “How strongly does curvature raise equilibrium vapor pressure?”

      A small droplet needs a higher vapor pressure to remain in equilibrium than a flat liquid surface. How much of that increase comes from radius, and how much comes from the condensing species and the assumed surface composition? We compare glycerol, squalane, and glycerol in an illustrative mixed phase at 300 K. This is a Kelvin-factor calculation, not a droplet-growth simulation.

      Read this alongside the activity comparison: activity and curvature are separate factors in equilibrium pressure. Material constants below are illustrative fixed inputs, without temperature dependence or a fitted surface-enrichment model.

      Separate surface tension from molecular volume

      Section titled “Separate surface tension from molecular volume”

      The builder converts 0.81 g/cm³ to 810 kg/m³ and 28 mN/m to 0.028 N/m for squalane. Its surface tension is lower than glycerol’s, but its is larger. Compare the product , rather than surface tension alone: these inputs give a larger Kelvin radius and factor for squalane at the same particle radius. The builder changes input handling, not the equation.

      Python · section 5

      Put two species in one assumed phase

      Section titled “Put two species in one assumed phase”

      The factory exposes the volume-weighted builder. Assign both species to phase zero explicitly: without a phase assignment, the strategy retains separate species surface tensions. Equal mass contributions of 0.1 kg/m³ give unequal volume fractions because the densities differ.

      This is an imposed homogeneous phase, not a prediction that glycerol and squalane are miscible. Volume averaging sets one surface tension for that phase. Each species still has its own molar mass and density. Calculate both species lanes, then select glycerol’s lane for comparison with pure glycerol. That comparison isolates the chosen surface-tension mixing rule. The pure squalane curve also changes the condensing species and is labeled separately.

      What the comparison tells us

      Section titled “What the comparison tells us”

      For these inputs, adding the lower-surface-tension component reduces the Kelvin factor for glycerol relative to pure glycerol. Pure squalane has the larger factor despite its lower surface tension, because its molar volume is larger. Thus surface tension alone cannot rank curvature effects across species. The equation checks verify parameter conversion and the imposed averaging rule; they do not establish that the mixture is physically homogeneous.

      For a research extension, vary the squalane mass fraction and compare glycerol’s at a fixed 20 nm radius under mass-, mole-, and volume-weighted surface tension rules. Keep the condensing species fixed. Measured mixture surface tension and phase behavior would be needed to choose among those approximations before using them in a condensation calculation.
