# Designing for a reader scanning study titles

A Particula TOC figure draws the eye to a study and makes its title easier to understand. These are design choices informed by research in adjacent fields. The studies below measured responses to advertisements and branding; they did not measure readership of Particula reports.

| Source and evidence | Application to a TOC figure |
| --- | --- |
| Pieters and Wedel tracked eye movements across 1,363 print advertisements. Pictures attracted initial attention across a range of image sizes. [Journal of Marketing, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkg.68.2.36.27794) | Spend the square on one distinctive picture. Let the adjacent title carry the verbal claim. |
| Luffarelli, Mukesh and Mahmood found that descriptive elements could make logos easier to process, with effects depending in part on brand familiarity. [Journal of Marketing Research, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022243719845000) | Give an unfamiliar study a recognizable cue from its own subject. In an ion study, a droplet can signal humidity. A generic atom could stand for almost any paper. |
| The National Park Service starts wayside design with the visitor’s surroundings and recommends small sketches to establish focus and hierarchy. [Harpers Ferry Center design guide](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/hfc/wayside-exhibit-design.htm) | Sketch the composition, then test the Particula figure at 2 × 2 inches with the actual title in place. See which relationship survives a quick glance before polishing the drawing. |
| ACS asks authors to consider the graphic and manuscript title together, limit text, and inspect the art at its output size. [ACS TOC graphic guidelines](https://pubsapp.acs.org/paragonplus/submission/toc_abstract_graphics_guidelines.pdf) | Keep the evidence plot available in the report. For the Particula listing, use the square dimensions in the skill. A journal submission may specify its own dimensions. |
