      From a reflecting surface to a satellite band

      A satellite looking toward sun glint can receive a much brighter signal than an instrument looking slightly away, even over ocean pixels receiving the same sunlight. Predicting these views requires a directional radiance. Calculating the energy leaving the ocean requires an integral over all upward directions. Both quantities follow from the surface reflection law and the atmosphere above it.

      Flux and directional reflectance

      Section titled “Flux and directional reflectance”

      For fixed wavelength, the bidirectional reflectance distribution function has units of sr⁻¹. Use and for the positive cosines relative to the surface normal in the incident and viewing hemispheres. With incident diffuse radiance and incident direct-normal irradiance evaluated at the ground, the reflected radiance toward is

      For a direct beam the directional-hemispherical reflectance is . Energy conservation requires for a passive surface. A Lambertian surface with albedo has because the cosine-weighted hemisphere integrates to steradians. For example, a dusty soil assigned under 500 W/m² downwelling in a band returns 150 W/m² upwards. Under the same Lambertian assumption its band radiance is 47.75 W m⁻² sr⁻¹ in every upward direction. An actual soil can change its apparent reflectance with both illumination and view.       Wind changes the probability that an ocean surface facet has the slope connecting the solar and viewing directions by specular reflection. A Cox–Munk model weights Fresnel reflection at that facet by its slope probability. Both the peak brightness and its angular extent vary with wind, so an angular quadrature must resolve the peak before its integral can be used as an albedo.
